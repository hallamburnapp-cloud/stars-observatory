# Methodological Note on the Orbital Governance Simulator as a Demonstrative Artifact

**Prepared for supervisory discussion — PhD thesis on State preventive obligations under Articles VI and IX of the Outer Space Treaty for AI-enabled harmful interference with satellite systems**
**University of Aberdeen, School of Law**

---

## 1. Purpose of this note

This note sets out the methodological status of the 3D orbital governance simulator built as a supporting artifact for the thesis, and specifically for the final chapter's proposed "STARS Framework" for operationalising Articles VI and IX preventive obligations. It anticipates questions the supervisor, and in due course examiners, are likely to raise about what kind of evidence — if any — a computational artifact can contribute to a doctrinal international law thesis. In short: the simulator is not evidence in the scientific or forensic sense; it is a **demonstrative and heuristic device** that renders visible, for doctrinal argument, structural features of the current space governance regime otherwise buried in dispersed registries, treaty text and technical literature. Its epistemic function is illustrative, not probative.

## 2. What the simulator is, and what it is not

The simulator fuses three public data sources: CelesTrak's General Perturbations (GP) element sets, which track approximately 16,000 active satellites and are themselves derived from tracking performed by the 18th Space Defense Squadron (18 SDS) of the US Space Force ([CelesTrak](https://celestrak.org/NORAD/elements/), [Space-Track documentation](https://www.space-track.org/documentation)); CelesTrak's SATCAT, comprising 34,157 cataloged on-orbit objects; and Jonathan McDowell's General Catalog of Artificial Space Objects (GCAT), used to cross-reference UN registration status ([GCAT / Jonathan's Space Report](https://planet4589.org/space/gcat/), [New Space Economy on GCAT](https://newspaceeconomy.ca/2026/03/10/jonathan-mcdowells-gcat-and-why-it-matters/)). From this fusion the simulator generates four analytical panels and a replay of the September 2019 ESA Aeolus/Starlink-44 conjunction.

What this artifact is **not**:

- It is **not** an empirical study in the social-scientific sense of testing a falsifiable hypothesis against a sampled dataset with a defined error model. The "findings" it displays (34% of payloads unregistered; ~69% US Article VI exposure by payload count; SpaceX operating roughly two-thirds of active satellites) are descriptive tabulations of public catalogue data, not statistical inferences requiring confidence intervals.
- It is **not** a space situational awareness (SSA) tool and makes no claim to rival, replicate or improve upon national or commercial SSA capabilities (e.g. the US Space Surveillance Network, EU SST, or commercial providers such as LeoLabs). The underlying orbital data is a public, deliberately degraded subset of what such systems hold internally (Section 4).
- It is **not** a prediction engine for future conjunctions or a risk-assessment tool for operational decision-making. The Aeolus/Starlink-44 replay is a historical reconstruction from published facts, not a forward-looking simulation of collision probability.

Its function is instead to **operationalise, in visible and interactive form, propositions that the thesis defends discursively** — namely, that the "space object" is an inadequate unit of legal analysis for AI-mediated risk, that "continuing supervision" under Article VI lacks operational content, that States lack timely operational control over the conduct that triggers their responsibility, and that AI systems compress the decision-time within which Article IX's "due regard" and consultation obligations must be discharged.

## 3. Methodological situation: empirically-informed doctrinal scholarship

The thesis is, and remains, a work of doctrinal international law: its central claims are normative interpretations of treaty text (Articles VI and IX of the [Outer Space Treaty](https://www.unoosa.org/oosa/en/ourwork/spacelaw/treaties/introouterspacetreaty.html)) supported by travaux préparatoires, State practice, and juristic opinion. The simulator sits within what might be termed **empirically-informed doctrinal scholarship**, or a socio-legal supplement to treaty interpretation: a mode of legal research that uses external, non-legal data not to generate legal rules inductively, but to test the descriptive premises on which a doctrinal argument depends, and to communicate the scale of a regulatory gap that words alone tend to understate.

This mode has clear precedent. Quantitative and computational artifacts have a growing, if still marginal, place in legal scholarship. Randal Picker's "SimLaw" project used agent-based computer simulation to explore how organised decision-making departs from individual rational-actor assumptions underlying law-and-economics doctrine, explicitly framing simulation as a way of making visible dynamics that textual or purely doctrinal analysis could not easily surface ([Picker, "SimLaw 2011", University of Chicago](https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2720&context=journal_articles); discussed in [McAdams, Illinois Law Review symposium](https://illinoislawreview.org/wp-content/ilr-content/articles/2002/4/McAdams.pdf)). More recent work on agent-based modelling as a "legal theory tool" argues explicitly that ABM allows legal scholars to generate "social systems artefacts that can be observed, evaluated, and tested" in ways that complement, rather than substitute for, doctrinal and law-and-economics analysis ([NYU Engelberg Center, "Agent-Based Modeling as a Legal Theory Tool"](https://nyuengelberg.org/outputs/agent-based-modeling-as-a-legal-theory-tool/); [Exploring the intersections between law, ABM and policy-making, CEUR-WS](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3182/paper16.pdf)). A parallel literature on "computational legal empiricism" in socio-legal studies similarly frames simulation and computational artifacts as tools for exploring legal phenomena that resist purely textual or statistical treatment, while stressing that such artifacts remain heuristic rather than dispositive ([Agent-based modeling and the interplay between cognition, society and law, Oñati Socio-Legal Series](https://opo.iisj.net/index.php/osls/article/download/2311/2610/15055)). Surveys of "computational legal studies" similarly distinguish "law-as-code" and "law-as-data" approaches from doctrinal argument, treating them as complementary evidentiary supplements rather than replacements ([SSRN, "Computational Legal Studies Comes of Age"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4826144)). The orbital simulator belongs to this same family: a bespoke, single-purpose data visualisation built to substantiate a doctrinal claim about regulatory gaps, not a generalisable predictive model.

Space law scholarship itself already draws on quantitative registration data to make doctrinal arguments about compliance gaps — for example, work quantifying registration delay and non-registration rates by State to argue that the Registration Convention's transparency objective is only partially realised ([Jakhu, Jasani and McDowell, "Critical issues related to registration of space objects", King's College London](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/files/83047469/Critical_issues_related_to_JAKHU_Publishedonline8December2017_GREEN_AAM_CC_BY_NC_ND_.pdf); [Lemay et al., graph-database analysis of registration timeliness](https://www.scribd.com/document/479492616/LeMayetal-2020-Representingandqueryingspaceobjectregistrationdatausinggraphdatabases-pdf)). The simulator situates the thesis within this established tradition of quantitatively-supplemented space law doctrine, rather than inventing a new genre.

## 4. Data provenance and honest limitations

Methodological candour requires the thesis to document, prominently, the limitations of each data source.

**CelesTrak / 18 SDS GP data.** All orbital elements ultimately derive from tracking performed by the US Space Force's 18th Space Defense Squadron via the Space Surveillance Network, disseminated publicly through Space-Track.org and mirrored by CelesTrak ([CelesTrak documentation](http://celestrak.org/NORAD/documentation/); [Space-Track help documentation](https://www.space-track.org/documentation)). This entails several material limitations: (i) Two-Line Element (TLE) / GP data encode *mean*, not osculating, orbital elements fitted to a specific propagation model (SGP4), and are subject to positional error that empirical studies place at roughly 1–2 kilometres or worse for uncooperatively tracked objects, improving to the tens-of-metres range only where operator-supplied ephemerides are used to generate "supplemental" TLEs ([Constant et al., AMOS Technical Papers 2023 on TLE accuracy limitations](https://amostech.com/TechnicalPapers/2023/Poster/Constant.pdf); [CelesTrak documentation on NORAD element set fidelity](http://celestrak.org/NORAD/documentation/)); (ii) the public catalogue is a deliberately filtered version of what 18 SDS tracks internally — classified payloads, certain national-security assets, and "analyst objects" with insufficient tracking fidelity are excluded or withheld from the public SATCAT ([NASA CARA FAQ on analyst objects](https://www.nasa.gov/cara/frequently-asked-questions/); [Popular Mechanics on declassification of previously withheld TLEs](https://www.popularmechanics.com/space/satellites/a25562991/pentagon-declassifying-space-traffic-data/)); (iii) the catalogue is a snapshot, decaying in accuracy between updates and undergoing structural transition as the five-digit catalogue-number space is exhausted in 2026 ([CelesTrak notice on catalogue-number exhaustion](https://celestrak.org/NORAD/elements/)). The simulator's population counts (~16,000 active satellites; 34,157 cataloged objects) should therefore be read as a lower-bound, public-domain approximation of the true on-orbit population, not a complete census.

**GCAT.** Jonathan McDowell's General Catalog of Artificial Space Objects is a meticulously maintained, but privately compiled and unofficial, cross-reference resource used here solely to link SATCAT/GP entries to UN registration status ([GCAT documentation, planet4589.org](https://planet4589.org/space/gcat/); description in [New Space Economy](https://newspaceeconomy.ca/2026/03/10/jonathan-mcdowells-gcat-and-why-it-matters/)). It is not itself an intergovernmental record and inherits any errors or omissions in its source material, though McDowell's own published work on registration adherence is among the most methodologically careful in the field ([McDowell, "Adherence to the 1976 Convention on Registration"](http://planet4589.org/space/un/un_paper1_14.html)).

**Registration lag.** The 34% non-registration figure must be read against the Registration Convention's own silence on any deadline: Article IV requires States of registry to furnish particulars only "as soon as practicable," a standard that in practice produces registration delays ranging from months to several years ([Jakhu, Jasani and McDowell, KCL](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/files/83047469/Critical_issues_related_to_JAKHU_Publishedonline8December2017_GREEN_AAM_CC_BY_NC_ND_.pdf); [Lemay et al. on median registration delay by State](https://www.scribd.com/document/479492616/LeMayetal-2020-Representingandqueryingspaceobjectregistrationdatausinggraphdatabases-pdf)). Some proportion of the apparent 34% "gap" is therefore lag rather than permanent non-compliance, and the figure should be presented in the thesis with this caveat attached at first mention and in any summary table.

**Attribution conventions.** The ~69% US Article VI exposure figure is calculated by payload count using SATCAT/GP "owner" or "launching State" fields, a convention that elides the more complex multi-State launching-State determinations the Registration Convention itself contemplates (Article II(2)) and that has generated its own scholarly gap literature, notably on joint-launch registration failures such as NSS-6 and NSS-7 ([Listner/Su, "Lost in Space? Gaps in the International Space Object Registration Regime", EJIL:Talk!](https://www.ejiltalk.org/lost-in-space-gaps-in-the-international-space-object-registration-regime/)). The figure is a proxy for concentration of responsibility, not a legal determination of liability in any specific case.

## 5. What each panel demonstrates for the Article VI/IX argument and the STARS Framework

**Panel 1 — Population and ownership concentration** (SpaceX operating roughly 10,748 of ~16,000 active satellites, approximately two-thirds of the active population) demonstrates empirically the thesis's claim that the "unit of risk" in contemporary orbital operations is the *integrated satellite system* — a single operator's constellation, sharing software, autonomous collision-avoidance logic and ground-segment decision architecture — rather than the discrete "space object" that Articles VII–IX and the Liability and Registration Conventions take as their conceptual unit. A regime built around individual object-by-object attribution struggles doctrinally when one non-governmental actor's AI-mediated fleet-management system governs thousands of objects as a single behavioural entity.

**Panel 2 — Registration cross-reference** (6,580 of 19,298 payloads, 34%, lacking a UN registration record) demonstrates, subject to the registration-lag caveat in Section 4, that the transparency infrastructure Article VI's "continuing supervision" duty implicitly relies upon — knowing what is in orbit, under whose authority — is itself structurally incomplete. This directly supports the thesis's claim that "continuing supervision" is content-thin: a State cannot meaningfully supervise activities of objects that are neither timely nor completely disclosed to the very institutional mechanism (the UN Register) designed to render supervision internationally visible.

**Panel 3 — National responsibility concentration** (~69% of payloads attributable to US Article VI responsibility) demonstrates the practical asymmetry between the diffuse, multilateral drafting assumptions of Article VI (a treaty negotiated when a handful of States conducted space activities) and a present reality where preventive obligations concentrate overwhelmingly on the domestic regulatory capacity of one or two States, principally exercised over non-governmental commercial actors whose AI-enabled operational decisions occur at machine speed. This grounds the STARS Framework's argument for differentiated, capacity-sensitive operationalisation of "continuing supervision" rather than a uniform standard.

**Panel 4 — Aeolus/Starlink-44 conjunction replay** reconstructs the September 2019 near-encounter between ESA's Aeolus satellite and SpaceX's Starlink-44, in which the collision probability rose from roughly 1-in-50,000 to 1-in-1,000 — ten times ESA's own manoeuvre threshold — while a software fault in SpaceX's on-call paging system meant the Starlink operator never saw the updated risk estimate, leaving ESA to manoeuvre unilaterally ([ESA operations account reported in SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/esa-urges-automated-satellite-collision-avoidance-systems-after-aeolus-starlink-maneuver/); [Forbes report of Holger Krag's account](https://www.forbes.com/sites/jonathanocallaghan/2019/09/02/spacex-refused-to-move-a-starlink-satellite-at-risk-of-collision-with-a-european-satellite/); [SpaceX's statement reported by Space.com](https://www.space.com/spacex-starlink-esa-satellite-collision-avoidance.html); [GeekWire coverage of ESA's blog posting](https://www.geekwire.com/2019/esa-shifts-spacecraft-avoid-starlink-satellite-spacex-reports-bug-collision-warning-system/)). This is deployed in the thesis as an Article IX case study in decision-time compression: the interval between risk-relevant data update and the point by which a manoeuvre decision had to be executed was measured in hours, not the days or weeks that inter-State consultation envisaged by Article IX's "appropriate international consultations" would ordinarily require. The episode illustrates, with a documented factual record, the thesis's core claim that AI-mediated operational tempo has outpaced the diplomatic and decisional cadence that Article IX's due-regard and consultation architecture presupposes — and thus motivates the STARS Framework's proposal for pre-authorised, automated escalation thresholds as a way of preserving meaningful State oversight within compressed decision windows.

## 6. Anticipated examiner objections and responses

*Objection 1: "This is data journalism, not legal research."* Response: the simulator does not purport to establish legal conclusions from data; it establishes descriptive premises (population concentration, registration incompleteness, decision-time compression) that the doctrinal chapters then subject to legal analysis. The distinction mirrors accepted practice in comparative and empirical legal scholarship, where quantitative description of a regulatory landscape precedes, and is analytically separate from, normative argument about that landscape's adequacy ([Computational Legal Studies Comes of Age, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4826144)).

*Objection 2: "The data is incomplete, so the findings are unreliable."* Response: incompleteness is disclosed as a feature, not concealed as a flaw (Section 4). The thesis's argument does not depend on precise figures but on order-of-magnitude claims — that a substantial, non-trivial proportion of the on-orbit population is unregistered, and that responsibility is heavily concentrated — which are robust to the margins of error involved and are corroborated by independent scholarship using overlapping but distinct methodologies ([Jakhu, Jasani and McDowell](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/files/83047469/Critical_issues_related_to_JAKHU_Publishedonline8December2017_GREEN_AAM_CC_BY_NC_ND_.pdf); [Lemay et al.](https://www.scribd.com/document/479492616/LeMayetal-2020-Representingandqueryingspaceobjectregistrationdatausinggraphdatabases-pdf); [EJIL:Talk!](https://www.ejiltalk.org/lost-in-space-gaps-in-the-international-space-object-registration-regime/)).

*Objection 3: "You are claiming your simulator out-performs classified SSA systems."* Response: no such claim is made, and the note above expressly disclaims it. The artifact's value lies precisely in showing what is knowable from the *public* record available to non-State observers, other States, and civil society — which is itself the relevant baseline for assessing whether Article VI's transparency-dependent "continuing supervision" duty is operable by any actor other than the small number of States with access to classified tracking data.

*Objection 4: "A one-off historical replay of a single incident cannot support a generalisable doctrinal claim."* Response: Aeolus/Starlink-44 is deployed as an illustrative case study, in the established common-law and doctrinal tradition of the exemplary case, not as a statistical sample. Its evidentiary weight rests on its being the first publicly documented instance of an active-satellite collision-avoidance manoeuvre against a mega-constellation member, and on the fact pattern being independently corroborated by ESA, SpaceX and contemporaneous press accounts ([SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/esa-urges-automated-satellite-collision-avoidance-systems-after-aeolus-starlink-maneuver/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/09/03/near-miss-between-science-craft-and-starlink-satellite-shows-need-to-improve-orbital-coordination/); [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2019/09/02/133180/one-of-spacexs-starlink-satellites-almost-collided-with-a-weather-forecasting-satellite/)), not on volume of repetition.

*Objection 5: "Why build a 3D simulator at all, rather than a table?"* Response: the visual and interactive form is itself part of the argument. Article VI and IX's obligations are frequently discussed in the abstract; rendering the actual scale and density of the constellation, and the ownership concentration within it, communicates a claim about regulatory salience that tabular data can understate. This follows the rationale articulated in the ABM-in-legal-theory literature for treating computational artifacts as tools that make emergent, systemic features of a regulatory environment newly visible to doctrinal analysis ([NYU Engelberg Center](https://nyuengelberg.org/outputs/agent-based-modeling-as-a-legal-theory-tool/)).

## 7. Conclusion

The simulator's contribution to the thesis is heuristic and expository, not evidentiary. It converts publicly available but institutionally fragmented data — CelesTrak/18 SDS tracking data, the SATCAT, and McDowell's GCAT cross-reference — into a single demonstrative artifact that renders visible the structural mismatches between Articles VI and IX's drafting-era assumptions and the present reality of AI-mediated mega-constellation operations. Every descriptive figure it displays is documented, caveated and traceable to public sources; every doctrinal conclusion the thesis draws from it is defended independently through conventional treaty interpretation, State practice and juristic authority. Read this way, the artifact strengthens rather than substitutes for the doctrinal argument, and situates the thesis within an established, if still emerging, tradition of empirically-informed and computationally-supplemented international legal scholarship.
