Article VI OST. States Parties bear international responsibility for national activities in outer space, ‘whether such activities are carried on by governmental agencies or by non-governmental entities’, and the activities of non-governmental entities ‘shall require authorization and continuing supervision by the appropriate State Party to the Treaty’ (emphasis added). The chart shows how the public catalogue distributes payloads across attributed States: a provision drafted when a handful of States conducted space activities now sits beside a catalogue in which well over ten thousand payloads have one attributed State. The attributed State is the SATCAT owner code, used as an evidentiary proxy only — not the launching State, the State of registry, or a determination of the ‘appropriate State Party’ under Article VI (see Provenance).

The Registration Convention (art IV) requires that information be furnished to the United Nations "as soon as practicable" and sets no deadline. This panel shows how far the public UN Register currently runs behind the orbital catalogue.

A missing match is not a finding of non-compliance. An object may be registered by a different launching State (art II(2)), by an intergovernmental organisation (art VII), or following a transfer of ownership (UNGA Res 62/101); matching against GCAT's UN registration field has its own limits; and States not party to the Convention may register under UNGA Res 1721 B (XVI).

Registration Convention (1975), arts II and IV. A launching State ‘shall register the space object by means of an entry in an appropriate registry which it shall maintain’ (art II(1)), and each State of registry must furnish its particulars to the UN Secretary-General ‘as soon as practicable’ (art IV(1)); non-parties register under UNGA Res 1721 B (XVI). The registry is the foundational transparency instrument the OST architecture presupposes — fixing the State of registry, which retains jurisdiction and control under Article VIII OST, and giving Articles VI and IX a factual anchor, although the State of registry need not be the State internationally responsible under Article VI. Where it demonstrably trails the catalogue, that doctrinal machinery loses its factual anchor.

Scope — managing expectations

What this instrument demonstrates
  • The scale and distribution of the tracked orbital population across attributed States (SATCAT owner code, an evidentiary proxy) — computed live from the public catalogue, never hand-drawn.
  • Registration lag: the measurable share of on-orbit payloads with no matching UN record in GCAT (a per-object proxy for the UN Register).
  • Registration lag as a time series: a daily ledger of how long objects remain catalogued but not yet matched to a UN record.
  • Documented incident chronologies relevant to Article IX, each step traceable to the primary and secondary sources cited below (OSCOLA 5).
  • Reproducibility: every catalogue-derived figure is recomputed daily by an open pipeline from cited public data; case-study facts and treaty status are dated and sourced.
What it does not do
  • No operational awareness: it does not predict conjunctions, issue warnings, or substitute for SSA services.
  • No real-time truth: positions are SGP4 propagations of daily element sets, carrying km-scale error.
  • Historical replays use archival general-perturbations element sets from the US Space Surveillance Network — km-scale accuracy, not the operator/CDM precision on which real decisions were made. The replay of each case study is evidence of geometry and chronology, not a conjunction-assessment tool.
  • No complete population: classified, untracked, and sub-10 cm objects (est. 1.2 million+ fragments ≥ 1 cm) are absent.
  • No legal determinations: registration and attribution fields are evidence for analysis, not legal findings, and nothing here is legal advice.

Attribution and matching rules

The attributed State is the owner code assigned in the public satellite catalogue. It is used as an evidentiary proxy only. It is not the launching State (Liability Convention art I(c); Registration Convention art I(a)), not the State of registry (Outer Space Treaty art VIII), and not a determination of the "appropriate State Party" under Outer Space Treaty art VI, which may be more than one State.

A payload is shown as having a matching UN record when McDowell’s GCAT records a UN registration document for it (the UNReg field). A missing match is not a finding of non-compliance.

Treaty party status is taken solely from UNOOSA’s annual status document and is shown with its document symbol and ‘as at’ date wherever it appears.

Limitations

  • TLE / SGP4 positions carry km-scale error and degrade with propagation age; this is not an operational conjunction-assessment (SSA) system.
  • Untracked objects are absent from the public catalogue; classified payloads are generally listed but without public element sets, and some national-security objects are withheld altogether.
  • Sub-10 cm debris is largely untrackable — ESA estimates about 1.2 million objects between 1 and 10 cm, none of which are represented here.
  • UN registration submissions lag launch by months to years; a payload with no matching UN record may simply not yet be matched. A missing match is not a finding of non-compliance.
  • Ownership attribution follows the 18 SDS catalogue (SATCAT OWNER) convention and may differ both from the Article VI ‘appropriate State’ (typically the licensing State) and from the launching State(s) and State of registry under the Registration Convention.
  • UN registration status is taken from the UN registration references recorded in McDowell’s GCAT, an unofficial secondary catalogue; it is not read directly from the UN Register and inherits GCAT’s transcription lag and any errors.
  • 15,587 catalogued objects (largely inactive payloads, debris fragments and rocket bodies outside the CelesTrak groups this instrument loads) are counted in the statistics but not propagated in the 3D view.
This instrument fuses publicly available tracking and registration data with a legal-attribution overlay. It is a demonstrative evidence instrument supporting doctrinal analysis of State preventive obligations under Articles VI and IX of the Outer Space Treaty — not an operational SSA system. What it is

STARS Observatory is a demonstrative evidence instrument. Each day it takes the public catalogue of objects in Earth orbit and shows the legal layer that governs them: which State the catalogue attributes each object to, whether and when a matching entry appears in the United Nations Register of Objects Launched into Outer Space, and how documented incidents unfolded in time. It exists to make the factual predicate of a legal argument observable, quantified and citable.

What it is not

It is not a space situational awareness service. Positions are propagated from public element sets and carry kilometre-scale error; the instrument does not predict conjunctions or issue warnings. It makes no legal determinations. The attributed State is the owner code in the public catalogue, used as an evidentiary proxy: it is not the launching State, the State of registry, or a finding as to the "appropriate State Party" under Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty, which may be more than one State. A missing United Nations registration match is not a finding of non-compliance: the Registration Convention sets no deadline for notification, and objects may be registered by a different launching State, by an intergovernmental organisation, or following a transfer of ownership. The instrument shows how far the public record runs behind the catalogue; it does not judge why.

Purpose

The Observatory was built to support a doctoral thesis in international space law at the University of Aberdeen on the preventive obligations of States under Articles VI and IX of the Outer Space Treaty in relation to AI-enabled interference with satellite systems. It is independent, non-commercial doctoral research infrastructure, created and maintained by one researcher. It is not an official University of Aberdeen resource, and the views expressed are the author's own.

Method and sources

All data are public: CelesTrak general perturbations data (OMM JSON) and satellite catalogue data (CSV), Jonathan McDowell's General Catalog of Artificial Space Objects (CC BY 4.0), and, for historical replays, archived element sets from a publicly hosted mirror of Space-Track history. Treaty party status follows the United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs' annual status document. The full methodology, matching rules and known limits are set out under Provenance.
